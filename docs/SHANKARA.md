# Adi Shankaracharya — a study

Compiled 2026-09-23, before the acharya page is written. The Acharyas
section starts with Shankara because everything else on this site
already leans on him: the Upanishads are read here through the
commentaries he wrote, and "Tat tvam asi" — the site's own name — is
the mahavakya his tradition put at the centre.

Every claim below is attributed. Where the tradition and the
historians disagree, both are given, and the disagreement is named
rather than quietly resolved. That is not diplomacy; it is the site's
governing rule applied to a person instead of a verse.

---

## 1. The problem before any biography

**There is no contemporary record of Shankara's life.** The Stanford
Encyclopedia of Philosophy states it plainly: "There are no dependable
historical records of Śaṅkara's life beyond his written works."

What exists instead is a genre — the *Śaṅkaravijaya*, "the conquest of
Shankara" — and every surviving example of it was written **five
centuries or more after he died**:

| Hagiography | Author | Date |
|---|---|---|
| *Bṛhat-Śaṅkara-Vijaya* | Citsukha | oldest; survives only in fragments |
| *Śaṅkaravijaya* | Anandagiri | — |
| *Cidvilāsīya Śaṅkara Vijayaṃ* | Cidvilāsa | 15th–17th c. |
| *Keraḷīya Śaṅkara Vijayaṃ* | anonymous, Kerala | c. 17th c. |
| *Śaṅkaradigvijaya* | Mādhava / Vidyāraṇya | 14th c., most cited |

The most-cited of them is not neutral. Vidyāraṇya was the twelfth
Jagadguru of Sringeri (in office 1380–1386) and a figure in the
Vijayanagara court; the account he shaped presented Shankara as the
teacher who harmonised the Hindu traditions, at a moment when that
claim had institutional and political use. The historical Shankara was
**relatively obscure until roughly the 14th century**, when the
hagiographies made him the heroic figure now taken for granted.

So this study separates three things throughout, and the page must
too:

1. **What his own writing shows** — the firm ground.
2. **What the tradition holds** — old, coherent, and believed, but not
   contemporary evidence.
3. **What historians have concluded** — itself contested.

## 2. When he lived — four answers, none of them certain

| Position | Dates | Held by |
|---|---|---|
| The mathas' own traditions | 5th c. BCE | Kanchi 509 BCE; Govardhana 507; Dvaraka 491; Jyotirmath 485; Puri 484; Sringeri 483 |
| Early Indology | **788–820 CE** | Tiele, and after him Max Müller, Macdonell, Deussen, Radhakrishnan |
| Nakamura (1983) | **700–750 CE** | argued from philosophical lineage and textual dependence |
| Potter (1981) | late 7th – early 8th c. | argued from Maṇḍana Miśra's dating |

Late 20th- and early 21st-century scholarship "tends to place
Shankara's life in the first half of the 8th century," and c. 700–750
CE is now the common scholarly estimate. The 788–820 dates, still
repeated everywhere, are the ones modern work has moved away from.

One strand of evidence for the earlier range: a Sringeri record places
his birth in the reign of a Vikramaditya, identified with **Vikramaditya
II of the Badami Chalukyas (r. 733–746 CE)**.

> **This affects the site today.** `seed/teachers.ts` currently prints
> `birth_year: 788, death_year: 820` and an era of "8th century CE".
> The century is defensible; the two precise years are the discarded
> estimate stated as fact. The page must not print them as though they
> were known.

The traditional lifespan is **thirty-two years**, and it is the one
biographical figure every account agrees on.

## 3. The life the tradition tells

Given as tradition, because that is what it is.

- **Kalady**, on the Periyar in Kerala, to an aged and childless couple
  who lived devoutly; his father died in his childhood. (Anandagiri's
  account instead places the birth at Chidambaram — the sources do not
  even agree on this.)
- **Sannyasa at eight.** Caught by a crocodile while bathing, the boy
  told his mother he would die a householder or live as an ascetic;
  she consented, and the crocodile let go.
- **The teacher.** He found **Govinda Bhagavatpāda**, the disciple of
  **Gauḍapāda**, on the Narmada at **Omkareshwar**. Gauḍapāda's
  *Māṇḍūkyakārikā* is the Advaita that existed before Shankara; the
  line of descent is the one part of the story with textual backing,
  because Shankara wrote a commentary on that very work.
- **Kashi**, then the writing of the commentaries, by tradition begun
  in his teens.
- **The debates.** He is said to have met the Mimamsakas — Kumārila,
  Prabhākara, and **Maṇḍana Miśra**, whose wife **Ubhaya Bhāratī**
  judged the contest, and who became the disciple **Sureśvara** on
  losing it.
- **The digvijaya**, the walking of the country, and the founding of
  the mathas.
- **The end at thirty-two.** Kedarnath is the usual claim; Kanchi,
  Kashmir and Kerala are also claimed.

### What scholars do with the Mandana story

Not much. The *Śaṅkaravijayas* "postdate Shankara by more than five
centuries" and are "filled with legends and fiction, often mutually
contradictory." On whether Maṇḍana and Sureśvara were one man:

- **Kuppuswami Sastri**: unlikely that the author of the *Brahmasiddhi*
  is Sureśvara, though the two were probably contemporaries.
- **R. Balasubramanian**: no conclusive evidence either way.
- **The doctrine itself argues against it** — Maṇḍana and Sureśvara
  disagree on where avidyā resides and on whether meditation helps
  towards liberation. Sringeri's own records identify them.

## 4. What he actually wrote

This is the firm ground, and it is firm because Paul Hacker and
Sengaku Mayeda built criteria for testing attribution rather than
accepting it.

**Accepted as his:**

- **Brahmasūtrabhāṣya** — the central work.
- **Bhāṣyas on ten principal Upanishads**: Īśā, Kena, Kaṭha, Praśna,
  Muṇḍaka, Māṇḍūkya, Aitareya, Taittirīya, Chāndogya, Bṛhadāraṇyaka.
- **Bhagavadgītābhāṣya.**
- **Commentary on the Gauḍapādakārikās.**
- **Upadeśasāhasrī** — the one non-commentarial work scholarship
  accepts.

**Doubted:** the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi*, whose authenticity "has been
questioned and mostly rejected by scholarship" — though it is the text
most often quoted as his in popular writing, and the site's own seed
data currently lists it among his key works. The Māṇḍūkya
commentary is also questioned.

**Attributed but not his:** over three hundred texts, "likely written
by admirers, or pretenders, or scholars with an eponymous name" —
which includes most of the beloved stotras. That matters directly for
this site's Stutis section: a stotra may be given "traditionally
attributed to Shankara" and not "by Shankara".

### The Upadeśasāhasrī, briefly

Two parts that the manuscripts suggest circulated independently: a
**metrical part of nineteen chapters** (ch. 1 on pure consciousness,
ch. 2 on negation — *neti neti*, ch. 18 on *Tat tvam asi*, the
longest), and a **prose part of three chapters** on how to teach,
corresponding to *śravaṇa*, *manana* and *nididhyāsana*. Its thesis is
compact: knowledge removes ignorance, and nothing else does.

## 5. The philosophy, in his own order

**Adhyāsa — the opening move.** The *Brahmasūtrabhāṣya* begins not
with God or the world but with an error: subject and object are "by
their very nature contradictory," like light and darkness, and yet we
mutually superimpose them — consciousness onto the mind-body, the
mind-body onto consciousness. *Adhyāsa* is "the apprehension of
something as something else." Everything after this follows from
naming that mistake.

**Brahman.** The nondual ground of all objects, the single
*adhiṣṭhāna* on which the universe depends. **Nirguṇa** — without
qualities — is Brahman as it is; **saguṇa** Brahman, Īśvara, is the
same reality as approached by a worshipper, and is finally set aside.

**Māyā and avidyā.** The world is "an objective but less-than-real
appearance," *māyā*, indeterminable as either identical with Brahman
or separate from it. *Avidyā*, ignorance, produces "a baseline of fear
and anxiety due to assuming one's self as a limited being subject to
sorrow, sickness, and death."

**Vivarta, not pariṇāma.** Brahman appears as the manifold world
**without undergoing any real change** — appearance, not
transformation. The world "is neither totally real nor totally
unreal."

**Three orders of reality:** *pāramārthika* (absolute), *vyāvahārika*
(the workable world, heavens included), *prātibhāsika* (the mirage,
the rope-snake).

**Identity.** Ātman is *numerically identical* with Brahman. The
individual has no power of projection of its own, and is not a part or
a spark of Brahman but Brahman.

**How it is known.** Six pramāṇas are accepted — perception,
inference, testimony, comparison, postulation, non-apprehension — but
Brahman "cannot be had by any means but through *śruti*", with *yukti*
(reasoning) and *anubhava* (experience) required alongside it. This is
why a commentator, not a system-builder, is the central philosopher of
the school.

**What he argued against:**

- **Pūrva Mīmāṃsā**: action cannot liberate, because action always
  presupposes a real individual agent — "a cause for bondage, not a
  means of liberation."
- **Buddhism**: he rejects Yogācāra idealism and Madhyamaka emptiness
  alike, holding that perceptual realism must be assumed at the
  workable level. The later charge that he was a *pracchanna bauddha*,
  a crypto-Buddhist, comes from his Vedantin critics, not from him.

**After him**, Advaita split over where avidyā resides: the **Bhāmatī**
school of Vācaspati Miśra and the **Vivaraṇa** school descending from
Padmapāda.

## 6. The institutions

Four cardinal mathas, each with a direction, a Veda, a mahavakya and a
first head:

| Matha | Direction | Veda | Mahāvākya | First head |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Govardhana, Puri | East | Ṛgveda | *Prajñānam brahma* | Padmapāda |
| Sringeri Śāradā | South | Yajurveda | *Aham brahmāsmi* | Sureśvara |
| Dvārakā Śāradā | West | Sāmaveda | *Tat tvam asi* | Hastāmalaka |
| Jyotirmaṭha, Badari | North | Atharvaveda | *Ayam ātmā brahma* | Toṭaka |

**The Daśanāmī orders** — Giri, Purī, Bhāratī, Vana, Āraṇya, Sāgara,
Āśrama, Sarasvatī, Tīrtha, Parvata — are the ten renunciate lineages
whose names a sannyasi takes. Their link to the four mathas
"remained nominal": many Daśanāmīs stand outside those institutions
altogether.

**Kanchi** is a fifth claimant to Shankara's foundation and is not part
of the four-matha scheme the other four recognise. The site should
name Kanchi as a major Advaita institution with its own tradition, and
not adjudicate the claim.

## 8. What the site will say, and will not

1. **Dates**: "first half of the 8th century, by the scholarly estimate
   now usual; the mathas' own traditions place him a thousand years
   earlier." Never a bare 788–820.
2. **Life**: told as tradition tells it, and labelled as tradition.
   Kalady, the crocodile, the debate with Maṇḍana, the death at
   thirty-two — none is presented as documented fact.
3. **Works**: three lists, not one — accepted, doubted, attributed.
   *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* moves out of "key works" and into "attributed".
4. **Philosophy**: in his own order, beginning with adhyāsa, because
   that is how he begins.
5. **Kanchi**: named, not adjudicated.
6. **No invented quotations.** "Brahma satyaṃ jagan mithyā" is a
   summary verse of the tradition, widely attributed to him; it is
   given as that.

## 10. The second pass — what deeper reading changed (2026-09-24)

The page was rewritten from an outline into a monograph. Four findings
from the specialist literature changed what it says, not merely how
much.

### Sundaresan on the hagiographies

Vidyasankar Sundaresan, *Conflicting hagiographies and history: The
place of Śaṅkaravijaya texts in Advaita tradition* (International
Journal of Hindu Studies 4.2, 2000: 109–84), is the study of the
genre. Its findings, in order of how much they matter here:

1. **"Śaṅkarācārya" had become a title** — borne by significant
   Advaita teachers and by heads of monasteries — long before any
   surviving hagiography was written. "It was no longer the name of a
   single historical person." Each author very likely wove in the
   legends of whichever titular Shankaracharyas he revered.
2. **This explains the contradictions** rather than excusing them.
   Anantānandagiri's Shankara is born at Chidambaram; Govindanātha's
   dies at Trichur; the *Sarvajñapīṭha* sits in Kashmir in one text and
   at Kanchi in another. These are plausibly **several men's lives
   under one name**, not one life corrupted.
3. **The 788–820 dates come from a hagiography**, and Sundaresan notes
   that internal evidence from the undisputed works points earlier in
   the eighth century.
4. **The Bṛhat-Śaṅkaravijaya ascribed to Citsukha** — routinely called
   the oldest — survives only in others' quotations, and he questions
   whether it ever existed.
5. Two seventeenth-century southern authors, Govindanātha and
   Rājacūḍāmaṇi Dīkṣita, **mention no mathas at all**.

### The mathas' early history is blank

Independently of the above: the early history of the Sringeri matha,
from the eighth century to about the fourteenth, **is unknown**, and
some historians argue the mathas as institutions date from that later
period rather than from Shankara's lifetime. What is documented starts
with Vijayanagara — the Vidyāśaṅkara temple completed **1338**,
tax-free lands granted **1346** under Bhāratī Tīrtha, Vidyāraṇya the
twelfth head in **1375**. Sri Bhāratī Tīrtha has been the
thirty-sixth Jagadguru since **1989**.

That is the oldest hard date in the whole institutional story, and the
page says so.

### The Brahma Sūtras, precisely

**555 sutras**, four adhyāyas of four pādas each, **189 adhikaraṇas**;
the chapters run samanvaya, avirodha, sādhana, phala. The text reached
its surviving form around 400–450 CE. It is deliberately compressed to
the point of being unreadable alone — which is why whoever supplies
the commentary supplies the philosophy, and why every Vedanta school
begins there and ends up somewhere different.

### The adhyāsa-bhāṣya, in its own words

The preamble's argument, as the translations give it: subject and
object "are opposed to each other as darkness and light" and cannot be
identified; superimposition is "the apparent presentation to
consciousness, by way of remembrance, of something previously observed
in some other thing"; the examples are mother-of-pearl and silver, the
double moon, the rope and the snake, the mirage; and "all our
experience starts in this error which identifies the Self with the
body, senses, etc." — worldly and Vedic activity alike.

The page quotes the opening sentence in Sanskrit, in the standard
printed text, with our own translation, and says so.
